## In short

- An employment contract matters most on the way out: when you resign, when you are dismissed, and in the months after you leave.
- The clauses to test are notice and pay in lieu, the non-compete, the training bond, and what happens to leave, bonus and commission you have already earned.
- Read those clauses together, not one at a time. The real cost usually comes from several of them applying to the same exit.

## Why the exit is where it matters

When you sign a job offer, you look at the salary, the title, the start date and maybe the probation period. Those describe the job while it goes well.

The rest of the contract describes what happens when it ends. That part is easy to skip, because on day one you are not planning to leave. Yet it is where an ordinary decision, like taking a better offer or turning down a move to another city, can turn into a debt, a lost bonus or a year you cannot work in your own field.

## Resignation notice

Most people expect to resign by giving the notice the contract states, working that period, and being paid until the last day. Things that can make it harder:

- **How notice must be given.** Some contracts accept only a signed letter, so a resignation sent by message or email may not count, and the notice period has not started.
- **Notice that grows with time**, or that is much longer for the employee than for the employer.
- **The employer ending the job at once.** If you resign with full notice and the employer tells you to leave today, a fair reading is that you are paid for the notice period. Some contracts say pay in lieu is at the employer's choice, or say nothing at all.
- **Penalties for short notice** that are larger than the pay for the missing days.

## Dismissal and probation

People generally accept probation, and accept that serious misconduct can end the job at once. What they expect beyond that is a fair reason for dismissal and, if the problem is performance, a warning and a real chance to improve.

Check how the contract defines misconduct, whether it requires a written warning first, and what notice or pay in lieu the employer gives. Watch for a list of "serious misconduct" so broad that almost any mistake fits, and for a rule that during probation the job can end without notice and without paying out leave or commission already earned.

A related trap is the unilateral change. Pay, role and place of work should not change without your agreement. Some contracts let the employer move the job to another city and treat an employee who refuses as having resigned, with no severance and the training bond still owed.

## Non-competes

A non-compete stops you working for a competitor, or starting a competing business, for a period after you leave. Most people expect one to be short, local, and limited to the same kind of work. Employment law in many countries also limits how far these clauses can reach.

Look at three dimensions:

| Question    | Narrow wording                       | Wide wording                                              |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| How long?   | A few months                         | Several years                                             |
| Where?      | The city or region where you worked  | "Any country where the company or its affiliates operate" |
| Which work? | The same role at a direct competitor | Any role at any business that "serves the same customers" |

Wide wording catches cases you would never think of as competing, such as a new job at a company that sells something different but to the same customers. Check also whether you are paid during the restricted period, and whether a separate clause stops you contacting former clients or colleagues.

## Training bonds

A training bond asks you to stay for a set period after the employer pays for training, or to repay part of the cost if you leave early. Most people find that fair, as long as the amount asked back is no more than the training actually cost and goes down for the time already served.

Watch for a fixed sum unrelated to the real cost, no reduction for months already worked, and wording that restarts the whole bond when a second course is added, even a short one you did not ask for. Check, too, whether the bond applies when the employer ends the job, not only when you resign.

## A worked example

Here is an illustrative case. The wording is invented, but each piece is the kind of clause you may meet.

Imagine a contract with clauses like:

- _"The Employee shall serve the Company for 24 months after completing any sponsored training. If the Employee leaves within this period for any reason, the Employee shall repay the full training cost."_
- _"Any bonus is payable only to employees in active service and not under notice on the payment date."_
- _"For 12 months after leaving, the Employee shall not be engaged in any business that serves the Company's customers, in any country where the Company operates."_

Now run an exit through it. The employee joins, completes a sponsored course, and fourteen months later the employer adds a two-day refresher. Ten months after the refresher, the employee resigns with full notice to take a job at a firm that sells a different product to some of the same customers.

What a reasonable person expects: the bond started with the first course and has almost run out, so little or nothing is owed. The bonus earned for the past year is paid. The new job is in a different line of work, so the non-compete does not apply.

What the wording does: the refresher counts as "sponsored training", so the 24 months restarted and the full cost is owed, with nothing taken off for time served. The employee is under notice on the bonus date, so the bonus is lost. The new employer "serves the Company's customers", so the non-compete applies, everywhere the company operates.

Each clause can look reasonable by itself. Together, one ordinary resignation costs a repayment, a bonus and a year of restricted work.

## What you have already earned

When the job ends, earned leave, bonus and commission are generally expected to be paid out. Check whether unused leave is paid or simply lost, whether commission on deals you closed is paid after you leave, and whether bonus depends on being "in service" on a later date. Look too at overtime: some contracts treat evening messages as part of the role, or give a short window to claim unpaid hours.

## Things you make

Many contracts say the employer owns what you create in the job. Some go further and claim anything you make while employed, including a side project built at home on your own laptop. If you build things outside work, read this clause closely.

## Questions

### How much notice do I have to give when I resign?

The notice your contract states, given the way the contract asks for it. Check whether a message or email counts, and whether the employer can end the job sooner and what it pays if it does.

### Is a non-compete clause always enforceable?

Not necessarily. Employment law in many countries limits non-competes, especially long or wide ones. safely.ink shows how far your clause reaches compared with what a reasonable person expects, and for Indonesia, Singapore and Australia it compares outcomes with the law.

### Do I have to repay the training bond if I leave early?

That depends on the wording. Check whether the amount matches the real training cost, whether it goes down for time served, and whether added courses restart the bond.

### What happens to my unused leave and bonus when I leave?

Many people expect earned leave, bonus and commission to be paid out. Some contracts make bonus depend on being in service on a later date, or let unused leave lapse.
