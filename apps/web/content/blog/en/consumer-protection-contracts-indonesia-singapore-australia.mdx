## In short

- All three countries limit what a business can bury in a contract it wrote alone and offered on a take-it-or-leave-it basis, but each does it differently.
- Indonesia bans specific kinds of standard clauses outright, Singapore tests many exclusion clauses for reasonableness, and Australia voids terms that are unfair under a three-part test.
- Each country has somewhere cheaper and less formal than a full court case to take a complaint, and financial products usually have their own route.

## Why standard-form contracts get special rules

Most contracts you sign as a consumer were written before you arrived: a phone plan, a gym membership, a loan, an app's terms of service. You can only accept or walk away, so lawmakers add rules for documents written entirely by one side.

This is a map, not a manual. Where we quote a law, the words come straight from the official page linked next to it.

## Indonesia: banned clauses that are void by law

The core law is [Law No. 8 of 1999 on Consumer Protection](https://peraturan.bpk.go.id/Details/45288/uu-no-8-tahun-1999) (Undang-Undang Perlindungan Konsumen). It calls a pre-written, one-sided clause a "klausula baku" (standard clause), and Article 18 deals with them directly.

Article 18(1) lists clauses a business may not use. One is a clause "menyatakan pengalihan tanggung jawab pelaku usaha" (stating that the business's responsibility is transferred away). Others say the business may refuse to take goods back or refund money, or make you accept new rules the business adds on its own while you use the service.

Article 18(2) covers how clauses look. A business may not use a standard clause whose "letak atau bentuknya sulit terlihat atau tidak dapat dibaca secara jelas, atau yang pengungkapannya sulit dimengerti" (placement or form is hard to see or cannot be read clearly, or whose wording is hard to understand).

The consequence is strong. Under Article 18(3), a clause that breaks these rules is "dinyatakan batal demi hukum" (declared void by law).

**Where to go.** Article 45 lets a consumer who suffers a loss bring a claim through a body that settles consumer disputes or through the general courts. Article 49 sets up the Consumer Dispute Settlement Body (Badan Penyelesaian Sengketa Konsumen, BPSK) at the regency or city level, and Article 52 says it settles disputes by mediation, arbitration or conciliation and also supervises the use of standard clauses.

**Financial services.** Banks, insurers, lenders and other financial firms are supervised by the Financial Services Authority (OJK). Its [OJK Regulation No. 22 of 2023 on consumer and public protection in the financial services sector](https://ojk.go.id/id/regulasi/Pages/Pelindungan-Konsumen-dan-Masyarakat-di-Sektor-Jasa-Keuangan.aspx) has its own list of banned exclusion clauses in Article 46(2). For example, a financial firm may not use a clause "menyatakan bahwa PUJK memiliki wewenang untuk menafsirkan arti perjanjian secara sepihak" (stating that the financial firm has the power to interpret the meaning of the agreement on its own). The list also covers clauses that limit your right to sue the firm or the evidence you may give in a dispute.

If the firm's own complaint process does not settle things, Article 82 of that regulation says you can complain to OJK, or take the dispute to an OJK-approved alternative dispute resolution body for the financial sector (LAPS Sektor Jasa Keuangan) or to court.

## Singapore: fair trading and the reasonableness test

The [Consumer Protection (Fair Trading) Act 2003](https://sso.agc.gov.sg/Act/CPFTA2003) targets unfair practices by suppliers. Section 4 says it is an unfair practice to mislead, to make false claims, or to take advantage of a consumer who the supplier knows, or ought to know, "is not reasonably able to understand the character, nature, language or effect of the transaction". Under section 6, a consumer who entered a transaction involving an unfair practice "may commence an action in a court of competent jurisdiction against the supplier", within a prescribed limit on the claim's value.

The [Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977](https://sso.agc.gov.sg/Act/UCTA1977) deals with clauses that exclude or limit liability:

- Section 2(1): nobody can use a contract term or notice to "exclude or restrict his liability for death or personal injury resulting from negligence". For other loss caused by negligence, the term must be reasonable.
- Section 3 applies where one party "deals as consumer or on the other’s written standard terms of business". The business then cannot rely on a term to escape liability for its own breach, or to perform in a way substantially different from what was reasonably expected, unless the term is reasonable.

Section 11(5) puts the burden on the business: "It is for those claiming that a contract term or notice satisfies the requirement of reasonableness to show that it does."

**Where to go.** Consumers often start with the Consumers Association of Singapore (CASE), which helps with complaints and negotiation. The Competition and Consumer Commission of Singapore administers the fair trading law. For a claim, the [Small Claims Tribunals Act 1984](https://sso.agc.gov.sg/Act/SCTA1984) governs tribunals for smaller disputes, with informal hearings.

## Australia: unfair terms are void

Australia's rules sit in the Australian Consumer Law, Schedule 2 to the [Competition and Consumer Act 2010](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00109/latest/text). Section 23(1) is direct: "A term of a consumer contract or small business contract is void if" the term is unfair and the contract is a standard form contract. The rest of the contract keeps working if it can operate without that term.

Section 24 sets the test. A term is unfair if it "would cause a significant imbalance in the parties’ rights and obligations arising under the contract", is not reasonably necessary to protect the legitimate interests of the business, and would cause detriment if relied on. Courts must consider transparency, including whether the term is "expressed in reasonably plain language", legible and readily available.

Section 25 gives examples of terms that may be unfair, such as terms that let only one side end or change the contract, or let one side "unilaterally to determine whether the contract has been breached or to interpret its meaning". Section 23 also makes it a contravention, with possible penalties, for a business to propose or rely on an unfair term in a standard form consumer contract.

**Financial products.** Loans, insurance and other financial products follow a parallel set of rules in section 12BF of the [Australian Securities and Investments Commission Act 2001](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00819/latest/text), which ASIC enforces.

**Where to go.** The ACCC's [guidance on contracts](https://www.accc.gov.au/consumers/buying-products-and-services/contracts) suggests first asking the business to remove or change the term, then contacting your state or territory consumer protection agency. The ACCC says it does not investigate individual complaints. For disputes with a bank, insurer, lender or super fund, the [Australian Financial Complaints Authority](https://www.afca.org.au/about-afca) describes itself as "a free, fair and independent dispute resolution scheme".

## The three countries side by side

| Country   | Main protection against unfair standard terms                                                                   | Where a person can go                                                           |
| --------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Indonesia | Law No. 8 of 1999, Article 18: listed clauses banned and void by law; OJK Regulation No. 22 of 2023 for finance | BPSK or the courts; for finance, OJK, LAPS Sektor Jasa Keuangan or court        |
| Singapore | Fair Trading Act for unfair practices; Unfair Contract Terms Act for exclusion clauses                          | CASE for help with complaints; Small Claims Tribunals for claims                |
| Australia | Australian Consumer Law, sections 23 to 25: unfair terms void; ASIC Act section 12BF for finance                | The business first, then a state or territory consumer agency; AFCA for finance |

The patterns are similar everywhere: one-sided change clauses, blanket exclusions, hard-to-read terms and limits on your right to complain all draw close attention. A clause you dislike is not automatically invalid, but you have grounds to ask, and somewhere to go if the answer is no.

## Questions

### Is an unfair clause automatically cancelled?

In Indonesia, a standard clause that breaks Article 18 is void by law. In Australia, an unfair term in a standard form consumer contract is void, though whether it is unfair may still need to be argued. In Singapore, many exclusion clauses cannot be relied on unless the business shows they are reasonable.

### Do these rules apply to online terms I clicked "I agree" to?

Generally yes. These laws look at what the contract says and how it was made, not whether it was signed on paper. A click-through agreement written by the business is a typical standard-form contract.

### Where should I start if I think a clause is unfair?

Start with the business, in writing, and keep a copy. If that fails, go to BPSK or OJK in Indonesia, CASE or the Small Claims Tribunals in Singapore, and a state or territory consumer agency or AFCA in Australia.

### Does the same law cover my bank or insurer?

Not always. Indonesia's OJK has its own regulation for financial firms, and Australia applies a parallel set of unfair terms rules to financial products through the ASIC Act.
