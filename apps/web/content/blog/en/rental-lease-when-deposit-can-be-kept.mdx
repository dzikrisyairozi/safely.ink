## In short

- A deposit is there to cover real damage and unpaid amounts. Most people expect it back at the end, less only damage beyond ordinary wear, with a list of what was deducted.
- The usual flashpoints are leaving early, worn versus damaged, repairs, a rent increase during the term, and how notice has to be given. A lease can quietly link several of them to the deposit.
- What the lease says is not always the last word. Tenancy or consumer law in many countries limits what a landlord may keep, so a harsh clause is worth checking, not just accepting.

## What a deposit is for

When you rent a place, the deposit is the landlord's security. If you leave owing rent, or leave the place damaged, the landlord can take what is owed from the deposit instead of chasing you for it.

That is the fair version, and most leases start there. The trouble comes from the extra clauses that widen what the deposit covers: forfeiting the whole amount for leaving early, treating every scuff as damage, or keeping it without any list. Each one can look small on its own. Together they can turn a refundable deposit into money you were never going to see again.

## When keeping some of it is usually fair

A reasonable person accepts paying for what they caused. Here is how common deductions tend to look from an ordinary tenant's point of view:

| Deduction                                       | How it usually looks to a tenant                       |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Rent still unpaid when you move out             | Fair                                                   |
| Repairing damage you or your guests caused      | Fair, at a reasonable cost                             |
| A cleaning fee the lease names up front         | Usually accepted                                       |
| A late fee stated in the lease                  | Usually accepted                                       |
| Repainting walls that faded over a long tenancy | Questionable: that is ordinary wear                    |
| Keeping the whole deposit for leaving early     | Questionable, especially if a new tenant moves in soon |
| Keeping it without saying what was deducted     | Questionable                                           |

The pattern is simple. Deductions tied to a real loss and a clear list feel fair. Deductions that work as a penalty, or that come with no explanation, do not.

## Wear and tear is not damage

Homes age while people live in them. Carpet thins in the hallway, paint fades where the sun hits, door handles loosen. That is ordinary wear, and most people expect the landlord to absorb it as a cost of renting the place out.

Damage is different: a burn mark on the counter, a hole in the wall, a broken window. Those are fair to charge for.

The trap is a lease that blurs the line. A clause like this one does exactly that:

> A clause like: "The Tenant shall return the Premises in the same condition as at the start of the tenancy. Any deterioration in condition shall be treated as damage and repaired at the Tenant's cost from the Security Deposit."

After three years, worn carpet and faded paint count as "deterioration", so the whole refurbishment comes out of your deposit. Read literally, the clause allows it. A reasonable person would not expect it.

## Leaving early

Life changes. A new job in another city, a family situation, a rent that has become too high. Leases deal with leaving early in very different ways, and the deposit is often where the cost lands.

Here is a worked example, using invented wording:

> A clause like: "If the Tenant ends this Lease before the end of the Term for any reason, the Security Deposit is forfeited in full and the Tenant remains liable for rent for the rest of the Term."

The scenario: the landlord raises the rent halfway through a fixed-term lease. The tenant decides they cannot afford it, gives the notice the lease asks for, and moves out. The landlord finds a new tenant within two weeks.

Under the clause, the tenant loses the whole deposit and still owes rent for every remaining month, even though the landlord is now collecting rent from someone else for the same place. Two separate costs land on one person for the same event.

What would most people expect? A tenant who leaves early owes rent until a new tenant is found, not always until the end of the term. The deposit pays for real loss and damage, not as a fine for leaving. And many would also ask whether the rent increase itself was allowed during a fixed term.

## Repairs: who fixes what

Most people expect the landlord to handle repairs that keep a place livable, such as a broken water heater, a leaking roof or failed wiring, within a reasonable time. The tenant handles small things and anything they broke.

Watch for these patterns:

- **"Minor repairs" with no limit.** A lease that puts all minor repairs on the tenant, without saying what minor means, can cover a repair that costs more than a month's rent.
- **No way to act in an emergency.** If water is pouring through the ceiling and the landlord does not answer, can you fix it and deduct the cost? Some leases forbid any repair without written approval, and then deduct the "unauthorised work" from the deposit.
- **No time limit for the landlord.** A duty to repair with no deadline leaves you with no hot water for as long as it takes.

## Rent increases, notice and the fine print

A few smaller clauses can also end up touching your deposit.

**Rent increases.** People generally expect the rent to stay put during a fixed term unless the lease says how and when it can change. A clause that lets the landlord raise it "at any time on notice" undoes the point of a fixed term.

**How notice must be given.** Some leases accept notice only by letter to a particular address. If you give notice by message and the landlord later says it does not count, your lease may roll on, along with your rent and the risk to your deposit.

**Automatic renewal.** A lease that renews on its own unless you object a set time before the end can lock you into another term if you miss the window.

**When the place cannot be lived in.** If a flood or fire makes the home unusable through no fault of yours, most people expect no rent for that time. A lease that keeps charging rent and holds the deposit as well deserves a close look.

**Entry.** Most people expect notice before the landlord comes in, except in an emergency.

## Questions

### Can a landlord keep my whole deposit if I leave early?

Some leases say so. Many people expect the deposit to cover real loss, such as rent until a new tenant moves in, rather than being forfeited as a penalty, and local law may limit what can be kept.

### What counts as ordinary wear and tear?

The gradual ageing of a home through normal use: thinning carpet, faded paint, loose fittings. Damage is something caused by an accident or carelessness, such as a burn, a hole or a broken window.

### Does the landlord have to tell me what was deducted?

Most people expect a written list of deductions with the remaining deposit. A lease that lets the landlord keep the deposit without any explanation is a common source of disputes.
