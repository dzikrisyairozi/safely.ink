## In short

- An NDA turns on two questions: how widely it defines "confidential information", and how long the duty lasts. A broad definition with no end date can cover almost anything you hear, for good.
- The usual exceptions carry a lot of weight: information that is already public, that you already knew, that you got from someone else or built yourself, or that the law makes you disclose. Check that they are written in, and how tightly.
- Your role changes what counts as a trap. A receiving party worries about fixed penalties for honest mistakes. A disclosing party worries about leaks by the other side's staff and files that never come back.

## Why NDAs feel harmless

A non-disclosure agreement is often the first document in a deal, and the shortest. You sign it so a meeting can happen, a job interview can go deeper, or a supplier can see your plans. The real deal feels like the thing that matters, so the NDA gets a quick look at best.

It matters later. It matters when talks end and someone asks for the files back, when a former colleague turns up at a competitor, or when an email goes to the wrong address. At that point, a few lines you skimmed decide who owes what.

## What counts as confidential

Everything starts with the definition. A narrow one covers only information marked "confidential" in writing when it was shared. A broad one reads more like this:

> A clause like: "Confidential Information means all information, in any form, whether or not marked as confidential, disclosed or made available in connection with the Purpose, including all notes, analyses and materials derived from it."

The broad version pulls in things most people would not think of as secret:

- something said out loud in a meeting and never written down
- the general know-how your team remembers after the talks
- a public fact that was mentioned in the same breath as a confidential one

None of this is wrong in itself. A disclosing party has good reasons to want broad cover. But the broader the definition, the more the exceptions and the time limit have to do.

| Definition style                                         | What it covers                                  | Who it tends to favour |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Marked in writing only                                   | Documents labelled confidential                 | Receiving party        |
| Marked, or confirmed in writing after an oral disclosure | Labelled documents plus confirmed conversations | Fairly balanced        |
| Everything shared, marked or not                         | Almost anything said or shown                   | Disclosing party       |

## The exceptions that should be there

Most well-drafted NDAs list the same carve-outs. Information is not confidential if it:

- is or becomes public, without any fault of the receiving party
- was already known to the receiving party before it was shared
- was received from someone else who was free to share it
- was developed independently, without using what was shared

A separate exception covers disclosure the law requires: a court order, a regulator's request, a legal duty to report. A reasonable version allows the disclosure and asks you to tell the other side first, where the law lets you.

The traps hide in the details. An exception may exist but demand written proof in a form nobody keeps. A legally required disclosure may be allowed only "with the prior written consent" of the other side, which you may not be able to get in time. Or the exceptions may simply be missing.

## How long the duty lasts

People generally expect a confidentiality duty to last a stated number of years, counted from when the information was shared or from when the agreement ended. Real trade secrets are the usual exception, and it is common for them to be protected for as long as they stay secret.

Two patterns deserve a second look. The first is a duty that lasts forever for everything, including ordinary business details that will be stale within a year. The second is a duty that runs "until the end of the agreement plus a period", in an agreement that has no clear end date. On paper the clock never starts.

## When an honest mistake meets a fixed penalty

This is where the definition, the time limit and the penalty clause come together. Take an invented example:

> A clause like: "Any disclosure of Confidential Information, whether intentional or not, entitles the Disclosing Party to a fixed sum of [a large amount] per occurrence, without any need to prove loss. The Receiving Party must notify the Disclosing Party immediately of any unauthorised disclosure."

Now the scenario. Talks ended years ago. Someone on your team forwards an old email thread to the wrong person outside the company. They notice, ask the person to delete it, and tell the other side two weeks later.

Read literally, the clause fires on every count. The information still counts as confidential because the definition is broad and there is no end date. The disclosure was an accident, but the clause covers accidents. Two weeks is not "immediately". The fixed sum is owed, with no need to show any loss.

What would a reasonable person expect? Most people accept that sharing confidential information on purpose should cost something real. Most would also expect an honest accident that is reported and fixed quickly to cost the actual loss, if any, rather than a fixed sum far above it.

## Use, sharing and giving things back

Beyond secrecy, most NDAs limit what the information may be used for, usually the deal or project named in the agreement. Using it later for a second project can be a breach even if nothing leaks.

Sharing inside your own organisation is normally allowed for employees and advisers who need the information and who are bound by the same duty. Some NDAs require written permission before you bring in an adviser. Sharing before the answer arrives can count as a breach.

At the end, the disclosing party can usually ask for everything back or destroyed. Pay attention to how far that goes:

> A clause like: "On request, the Receiving Party shall return or destroy all Confidential Information, including all electronic copies and backups, and certify this in writing within five days."

Automatic backups are often impossible to purge on short notice. A more workable version lets copies stay in routine backups, still under the confidentiality duty, until they are overwritten.

| If you are the... | Things that usually matter most                                                                                                                                      |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Receiving party   | A clear definition, the standard exceptions, a time limit, actual loss rather than fixed penalties for honest mistakes, a workable rule on backups                   |
| Disclosing party  | Use limited to the stated purpose, return of everything on request, the other side answering for leaks by its own staff and advisers, a duty that outlasts the talks |

In a mutual NDA both sides share and receive, so both lists apply. Also check whether the "NDA" quietly includes extra promises, such as not hiring each other's staff or not approaching each other's clients. Those are separate restrictions with their own costs.

## Questions

### Is information confidential if it was never marked as confidential?

It depends on the definition. Some NDAs cover only marked material, while others cover everything shared in connection with the deal, marked or not, including things said in meetings.

### Can an NDA last forever?

Some are written that way. Many people expect a stated number of years for ordinary business information, with longer protection only for real trade secrets.

### What if a court or regulator asks for the information?

Most NDAs allow a disclosure the law requires, often with a duty to tell the other side first where that is permitted. Check that your NDA has this exception and that it does not depend on the other side's consent.

### What happens if I share something by accident?

That depends on the wording: whether accidents are covered, how fast you must report, and whether a fixed penalty applies. If it has actually happened, speak to a qualified professional about your situation.

### Does safely.ink tell me whether to sign?

No. It shows how the wording plays out in realistic scenarios and where the outcome is worse than a reasonable person would expect. The decision stays with you.
